## What the dated player reports describe
Player discussions and reviews checked on November 4, 2026 described a recurring concern: damage that does not simply cost a few seconds, but follows the speeder into later races as Faulty Parts. One November 4 review, written after about eight hours of play, described having to carry "permanent, run-killing" Faulty Parts and asked why Hibi does not simply repair the speeder between events. A November 3 review from a player with almost 30 hours called the repair system abstract and said permanent damage felt inconsistent.

The reports below are individual observations, not a measured failure-rate study or a verified universal repair formula. Use the part and penalty descriptions in your current build to decide what is affected. This guide organizes a recovery decision; it does not claim that every crash or hazard produces a predictable malfunction.

## What players report breaking a part
The game does not publish a damage table, so treat the following as player reports, not exact rules. A Steam feedback thread from October 21 argued that malfunctions are both too disabling and too easy to get. The poster said a part bought at the first shop could malfunction before the second planet and effectively end the run. They singled out the acid planet that appears after qualifying, saying even half a second in acid while setting up a jump was enough. In the worst case they described, the malfunction disabled their booster and abilities for thirty seconds at the start of a race, which they estimated as an entire lap.

The November 3 review adds an important nuance. That player said a full crash into a wall could respawn them with no lasting damage, while a few seconds over acid could leave the vehicle damaged for the next three races, and sometimes not at all. In other words, players do not experience permanent damage as a straight function of how hard they hit something. Hazards that soak the speeder over time seem to be the common trigger in these reports, and the result is not always the same. A separate post in a busy General Discussions thread described a Quick Podrace in which repeated rams left the poster smoking with a system malfunction while their target looked unharmed. Whether that was luck, stats, or a mechanic they missed, it is a single report from that event, not proof that every mode applies the same malfunction rules.

If acid is your problem, use the <a href="/tracks/all-named-tracks/">track and hazard practice method</a> to review the approach before the next Tour.

## How repairs work, according to players
The most detailed recent description of repairs comes from the November 3 review. Rather than a droid fixing the speeder in a cutscene, the player said, you get a meter and push the button at the right moment, which they compared to an active reload in a shooter. They also said damage reads more like an ability or performance penalty than a physical injury to one side of the vehicle. Hitting a wall with the left side does not damage a particular left-side part.

Two practical takeaways follow from that report. First, if your current repair interface matches that description, read its prompt before acting and give the input your attention rather than rushing toward the next event. Second, base your response on the penalty actually displayed. A malfunction that locks your boost hurts a Ramjet-heavy build far more than one built around cornering, and the reverse is also true. Read what the part screen says is affected before you decide whether to keep it, repair it, or replace it.

## Plan shop visits around the part you cannot lose
Before opening the shop, identify the displayed penalty and the part or ability your next objective depends on. Do not clear the inventory simply because several options are available. Compare the effect, cost and alternative use of those resources. A purchase is useful when you can explain which problem it solves.

A practical priority is: Before you spend, decide which single part your build depends on. For a boost build it may be the Ramjet or Afterburner part; for a contact build it may be whatever keeps you upright through shunts. Then spend so that losing anything else would be survivable. A run with one critical part and several cheap, replaceable ones handles a malfunction far better than a run where every slot is equally essential.

In online Tours the time between rounds is short. An early November Steam thread asked for a way to mark favourite parts for each character, because there is not much time to judge whether the randomly offered parts suit your build or whether you should reroll. Until anything like that exists, make the decision before the shop opens. Know which stat you are buying for and skip the rest.

## Compare the current craft before changing pilots
Read the resilience and equipment information shown by the game, but do not convert a forum screenshot into a universal stat table. An old Quick Podrace snapshot does not establish every pilot's values in every mode or update. Compare the actual selections you can use for the event in front of you.

If your main failure is leaving the route, changing a stat may not address the first cause. Review the approach and the point where you lose control. If the craft stays on line but a displayed malfunction removes a critical function, inspect repair or replacement options before trying to drive the same plan harder.

Use the <a href="/vehicles/landspeeders/">class reference</a> to understand handling differences. Keep the next attempt interpretable: changing the pilot, class and several parts at once makes it difficult to know which change helped.

## Decide when the run is still worth finishing
A damaged run needs a fresh decision rather than an automatic restart. When a malfunction hits, ask three questions. Does it disable something your build needs right now? Can the next shop or repair remove it? And is the next planet one where hazards will make it worse?

If the answers are no, yes, and no, keep racing conservatively and take podium finishes over risky wins. If the penalty locks your main boost before a hazard-heavy planet, play it safe, protect what you have, and try to reach the next shop. If you start overcommitting to recover lost time, monitor boost warnings and keep enough room to finish the next section. ## Build a useful recovery record
Record the event, the part affected, the exact penalty displayed and the repair or replacement option you used. Add the result of the next attempt. A description such as “lost boost after the hazard, replaced the affected part, then completed the next event” is more useful than “the whole build is broken.”

If the same problem repeats, keep the setup fixed long enough to understand the trigger you can actually observe. Avoid publishing an exact exposure time from a single anecdote. If the interface and the result disagree, preserve screenshots and use the game's support route rather than deleting the save or restarting every time.

The October 22 quality-of-life update addressed party size, keyboard layouts and several stability issues; it does not establish a new malfunction formula. Recheck later patch notes when investigating a changed behavior, and keep dated player observations separate from confirmed changes.
