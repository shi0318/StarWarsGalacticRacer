## Why Faulty Parts are the complaint of the week
Ask players what ended their last campaign run and a growing number will not name Kestar Bool, a rival, or a hard corner. They will name a broken part. As of November 4, 2026, the newest English Steam reviews keep returning to the same mechanic: damage that does not simply cost a few seconds, but follows the speeder into later races as Faulty Parts. One November 4 review, written after about eight hours of play, described having to carry "permanent, run-killing" Faulty Parts and asked why Hibi does not simply repair the speeder between events. A November 3 review from a player with almost 30 hours called the repair system abstract and said permanent damage felt inconsistent.

The game itself confirms that Faulty Parts are a real system rather than a bug. Steam's achievement list includes **Hunk of Junk**, which asks you to place 1st in an event with at least four Faulty Parts installed. On November 4, 2026, Valve's global stats show only 5.2% of players had earned it. That number is useful context: winning while heavily damaged is possible, but most players do not do it. If your run is falling apart, you are in the same position as the majority of the player base, and the job is damage control rather than heroics.

## What players report breaking a part
The game does not publish a damage table, so treat the following as player reports, not exact rules. A Steam feedback thread from October 21 argued that malfunctions are both too disabling and too easy to get. The poster said a part bought at the first shop could malfunction before the second planet and effectively end the run. They singled out the acid planet that appears after qualifying, saying even half a second in acid while setting up a jump was enough. In the worst case they described, the malfunction disabled their booster and abilities for thirty seconds at the start of a race, which they estimated as an entire lap.

The November 3 review adds an important nuance. That player said a full crash into a wall could respawn them with no lasting damage, while a few seconds over acid could leave the vehicle damaged for the next three races, and sometimes not at all. In other words, players do not experience permanent damage as a straight function of how hard they hit something. Hazards that soak the speeder over time seem to be the common trigger in these reports, and the result is not always the same. A separate post in a busy General Discussions thread described a Quick Podrace in which repeated rams left the poster smoking with a system malfunction while their target looked unharmed. Whether that was luck, stats, or a mechanic they missed, it is another sign that malfunctions are a fact of life in every mode.

If acid is your problem, read our <a href="/tracks/sentinel-one/">Sentinel One guide</a> before the next tour. Most of the reports above point there.

## How repairs work, according to players
The most detailed recent description of repairs comes from the November 3 review. Rather than a droid fixing the speeder in a cutscene, the player said, you get a meter and push the button at the right moment, which they compared to an active reload in a shooter. They also said damage reads more like an ability or performance penalty than a physical injury to one side of the vehicle. Hitting a wall with the left side does not damage a particular left-side part.

Two practical takeaways follow from that report. First, the repair input is a skill check you can practise rather than a dice roll you have to accept, so do not rush through it while thinking about the next race. Second, because damage is a performance penalty, the right response depends on what the penalty is disabling. A malfunction that locks your boost hurts a Ramjet-heavy build far more than one built around cornering, and the reverse is also true. Read what the part screen says is affected before you decide whether to keep it, repair it, or replace it.

## Plan shop visits around the part you cannot lose
The achievement list shows how much of a run is spent shopping. **Treasures, Treasures!** asks you to buy every available part at the Scrap Dealer in a single visit (6.2% of players on November 4), and **Might as Well Buy a New Speeder** asks you to do the same at Hibi's Shop (3.8%). Those low rates suggest that clearing a whole shop is rare, so most runs are about choosing a few parts well. **The Real Soul of Racing**, for increasing two Upgrades to maximum level in a single tour, sits at 34.7%, and **The Fastest Ever Built**, for four Legendary Parts installed at once, sits at 13.5%.

That makes a simple rule worth following. Before you spend, decide which single part your build depends on. For a boost build it may be the Ramjet or Afterburner part; for a contact build it may be whatever keeps you upright through shunts. Then spend so that losing anything else would be survivable. A run with one critical part and several cheap, replaceable ones handles a malfunction far better than a run where every slot is equally essential.

In online Tours the time between rounds is short. An early November Steam thread asked for a way to mark favourite parts for each character, because there is not much time to judge whether the randomly offered parts suit your build or whether you should reroll. Until anything like that exists, make the decision before the shop opens. Know which stat you are buying for and skip the rest.

## Resilience and pilot choice change the odds
Resilience is the stat that reports keep coming back to. Our <a href="/tips/wreck-out-recovery/">wreck-out recovery guide</a> already argues for resilience before speed in the shop, and the malfunction reports above strengthen that case. If the damage that turns into Faulty Parts mostly comes from staying in hazards, a speeder that survives an extra second of contact gives you that second to steer out.

Pilot stats matter too. On October 29, a Steam user posted the stats they saw for every racer in Quick Podrace. All six showed Speed 857, but Resilience varied widely: Sebulba 9, Ody Mandrell 5, Dud Bolt 5, Gasgano 5, Teemto Pagalies 4, and Ben Quadinaros 2. If you keep losing pod races to damage, a sturdier pilot is a real option, and the same thinking applies when you choose a speeder and Racer Style in the campaign. ## Decide when the run is still worth finishing
Not every damaged run is lost. **Clean Sweep**, placing 1st, 2nd, or 3rd in every event of a single campaign tour, is held by 44.8% of players, which means plenty of runs reach the end despite setbacks. When a malfunction hits, ask three questions. Does it disable something your build needs right now? Can the next shop or repair remove it? And is the next planet one where hazards will make it worse?

If the answers are no, yes, and no, keep racing conservatively and take podium finishes over risky wins. If the penalty locks your main boost before a hazard-heavy planet, play it safe, protect what you have, and try to reach the next shop. Overheating is a separate risk worth keeping in mind: **Risk, No Reward**, for blowing up by overheating your Ramjet, has been earned by 84.5% of players. Pushing a damaged build harder to make up time is exactly how many of those explosions happen. Our <a href="/tips/ramjet-heat/">Ramjet heat guide</a> covers how to manage it.

## What to watch for next
Fuse Games has not mentioned Faulty Parts or malfunctions in any Steam patch notes up to November 4, 2026. The October 22 quality-of-life update focused on party size, keyboard presets, and stability, and the October 27 accolades post said the team will keep working from player feedback and share more about the game's future soon. If a later patch changes malfunction frequency or repair, it will appear in the Steam news feed first. Until then, plan around the system as it is: buy for one critical part, raise Resilience before hazard planets, take the repair meter seriously, and treat a 5.2% achievement as a reminder that winning on four broken parts is the exception.
